# Choosing a tuner gain automatically

Measurements from 12 and 13 September 2026, on an RTL-SDR Blog V4, against two
antennas at one station.  Written to explain why `[rtlsdr] arc_headroom_db` is 32 and
why the sweep works the way it does.

## What the calibration has to decide

Two numbers, and they pull against each other:

- **Too little gain** and the noise floor is the converter listening to itself, so the
  reported floor is too high and every SNR is compressed.
- **Too much gain** and an arc clips.

Clipping is the worse failure, and not only because a clipped arc reads smaller than
it is.  Clipping is nonlinear, so it puts products across the whole span and lifts the
apparent noise floor in the same capture.  Both numbers are then wrong, and nothing in
the data says so.  A converter-limited floor is wrong by a bounded amount in a known
direction.  So the headroom is a hard limit and the floor accuracy is whatever can be
had underneath it.

## Neither measurement may depend on a signal being present

This is the constraint that shaped everything else.  Nobody can promise an arc is
running when an operator opens the calibration tool, so anything measured from an
observed peak is worthless half the time.

Two measurements avoid it:

**Quiet broadband level.**  A low percentile of the per-frame RMS in the time domain,
so bursts are stepped over and what remains is the level between them.  It exists on a
dead band.  Reserving a fixed number of dB above it leaves room for an arc that has
not arrived.

**Per-bin noise floor against the converter's own.**  A low percentile across frames
and then across bins, so neither an arc nor a strong station lifts it.  This says
whether the antenna or the converter is what the station is measuring.

An observed peak and a clipping count still get recorded.  With an arc running
they confirm it fits the reserve.  Without one the answer is unchanged.

## Why the reserve is 32 dB

The station's own CSV logs decided it.  Over 11147 locked minutes:

    SNR range   3.07 to 30.95 dB, mean 13.21
    >= 15 dB    39.89%
    >= 20 dB    18.44%
    >= 25 dB     3.72%
    >= 30 dB     0.06%   (7 rows)
    >= 35 dB     0.00%

The loudest arc that station ever recorded reached 30.95 dB above its own noise floor.
Those logs came through a 4 kHz SSB filter, while clipping happens across the whole
256 kHz, where the crest of a burst runs a decibel or two higher.  32 dB covers the
worst logged arc and that difference.

A second pass over the whole of 2026 to date found nothing above 35 dB either, except
104 rows in April reporting about 128 dB.  Those are an artifact rather than a
measurement, and the number says which one: `amplitude_to_dbm` returns the
`SILENCE_DBFS` sentinel of -128 for an amplitude of zero, and `snr` is
`sig_dbm - noise_dbm`, so a noise floor of exactly -128.00 puts the SNR 128 dB above
whatever the signal read.  Check the noise floor column on those rows to confirm it.
A sentinel that survives arithmetic and reaches the CSV as a plausible-looking figure
is a defect in its own right, whatever the analysis here concludes.

What it costs, on the broadband antenna the figures below came from:

    reserve   max gain   antenna share of floor   floor reads high by
     25 dB      40.2             98%                   0.08 dB
     30 dB      37.2             88%                   0.56 dB
     32 dB      36.4             81%                   0.93 dB
     35 dB      33.8             71%                   1.46 dB

Moving from 30 to 32 costs 0.37 dB of floor accuracy.  Cheap, against an arc that
cannot be recovered once it clips.

## How much of the floor has to be the antenna

The reserve decides the highest gain.  A second bound decides the lowest, because a
gain too small leaves the converter making most of the noise the station reports.
`buzz.gain_sweep._ANTENNA_SHARE_FLOOR` is where that bar sits, and the sweep picks the
lowest gain that clears it.

At one half the antenna equals the converter and the reported floor reads 3.01 dB
high.  The bar is a choice rather than a measurement, and against the broadband
antenna above it decides the answer:

    bar    gain picked    reported floor reads high by
    0.5       32.8 dB               1.87 dB
    0.7       33.8 dB               1.54 dB
    0.8       36.4 dB               0.92 dB
    0.9       40.2 dB               0.41 dB

0.8 would reproduce the 36.4 dB this station settled on by hand.  0.5 was chosen
anyway, on the same reasoning as the reserve: clipping is the failure that cannot be
recovered from, so the spare 3.6 dB above the reserve is worth more than the decibel
of floor accuracy it costs.  A lower bar also finds an answer on quieter antennas
where a higher one finds none, and the antennas that need help are the quiet ones.

The two bounds can cross, and on a quiet antenna they do.  The sweep reports that
rather than picking a number from a rule that failed.

## The sweep has to interleave

A first attempt swept each gain once in sequence and produced neighboring steps
disagreeing by 16 dB, which is more than the gain between them.  The cause was not the
receiver:

    at a fixed 32.8 dB, 20 consecutive seconds:  floor spread 0.9 dB, sd 0.2 dB
    at the same 32.8 dB a few minutes earlier:   floor 7.4 dB higher, 357 clipped

The floor is steady second to second and unsteady minute to minute, because the arc
comes and goes.  A sweep takes about a minute, so every step is measured in a
different world.

The fix is several passes over the whole range, alternating direction, combined per
gain and differently for the two quantities:

- **floor: median across passes**, since it decides the knee and transients are noise.
- **peak: maximum across passes**, since the worst case is the only interesting one.
  A median here would size headroom for a quiet moment.

Five passes at a quarter second per step took about 75 seconds and gave a monotonic
curve where one pass had given noise.

### The number of passes has to be odd

Found in simulation afterwards, and it is a property of the combining rather than of
the receiver.  The floor takes a median, and numpy's median of an even count averages
the two middle values rather than picking one, so an even number of passes gives up
exactly the outlier rejection the passes were added to buy.

An arc running through half the sweep is the case that separates them.  Against a
simulated arc that lifts the band noise 7.4 dB for a stretch of the run, and asking
whether the sweep still reaches the answer it reaches on a quiet band:

    passes    steps    matches the quiet-band answer
       1        29              25 of 25
       2        58               0 of 25
       3        87              25 of 25
       5       145              25 of 25
       7       203              25 of 25

`GainSweep` rounds an even request up to the next odd number for that reason.

Two other things that table says.  Three passes would do in simulation, and five is
kept because the figure came from hardware rather than from a model, where the arc is
not as well behaved as any of this.  And one pass scoring 25 of 25 is a limit of the
model rather than a defence of one pass: the hardware measurement above is what one
pass actually did.

## What the two antennas showed

**A mag loop resonant at 3.530 MHz.**  Too quiet to swamp an 8-bit converter at any
gain.  Subtracting an off-resonance run at 14.200 MHz isolated what the antenna
delivered: 5.0 dB *below* the converter's own noise at the configured 40.2 dB gain,
reaching only 3.8 dB above it at maximum.  `clipped` was 0 at every gain including
maximum.

That explains an anomaly the operator had noticed weeks earlier and attributed to
saturation: about 10 dB between signal and noise through the receiver against 18 dB on
a KX3 on the same antenna.  It was not saturation.  The floor read high because it was
mostly converter noise, which compresses SNR from below.

**A broadband antenna.**  Live from 160m through 15m, and clipping readily: 16579
clipped samples on 160m at 44.5 dB gain, 1567 on 80m.  The knee is where the model
says, and the calibration settles on 36.4 dB with 81% of the floor coming from the
antenna.

The difference between the two is worth keeping in mind when this is built: a quiet
antenna has no gain that satisfies both constraints, and the tool has to say so rather
than pick a number.  "Your antenna is quiet enough that the noise floor will be partly
mine" is something an operator can act on.

## What could not be measured

**The true gain per step.**  The sweep measures differences, not absolutes: displayed
level is antenna noise plus gain plus a fixed unknown chain constant, and the two
unknowns cancel only in a difference.  Worse, on the mag loop four proxies for the
gain curve disagreed over the same span, from 0.99x to 1.36x, because each was
contaminated differently.  Nothing on the band was strong enough to settle it.  WWV at
5 and 10 MHz, shortwave at 6, 7.3, 9.7 and 15 MHz, and 1550 kHz MW were all tried; the
loop was deaf at every one, and the only steady signals found were internal spurs.

This is why the audio-to-RF offset estimate stays at the negative of the nominal gain
rather than a measured curve.  Not merely because the label error is under one S unit,
but because the curve frequently cannot be obtained at all.

The figure of 57.5 dB against a nominal 49.6, quoted in `config.py` and `sdr.py`, was
measured once on one unit and has not been reproduced.  Treat it as provisional.
