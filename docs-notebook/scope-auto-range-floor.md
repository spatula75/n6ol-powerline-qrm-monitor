# The scope auto-range floor after IQ conversion

`buzz.scope.auto_range_full_scale` limits how far the scope magnifies a quiet input.
The original limit was 32 int16 counts, or about -60.2 dBFS.  On 2026-09-18, an SDRplay
RSP1B on the rooftop antenna asked for scales between 2.84 and 31.05 counts.  The fixed
floor therefore hid useful detail during the quieter part of the run.

The first receiver-specific replacement used the ADC depth directly.  That model was
wrong because the scope never sees ADC samples. `IqToAudio` applies a one-sided FIR,
decimates the result, takes its real part, and scales it into int16 audio first.  The FIR
averages uncorrelated quantization noise, so an 8-bit input no longer arrives at the
scope in 256-count steps.

## The quantity used now

`SdrDevice.effective_bits` states the resolution delivered by the receiver library.
`IqToAudio.processing_gain_bits` derives the extra noise resolution from the actual
filter taps:

```text
noise gain = sqrt(sum(abs(taps) ** 2))
processing gain in bits = -log2(noise gain)
```

`SdrPipeline.effective_bits` adds those figures and caps the result at the 16-bit audio
container. `buzz.scope.minimum_full_scale` then converts the combined resolution into
int16 counts.  This keeps the hardware fact, conversion fact, and display policy in the
objects that own them.

At the default 256 kHz IQ rate, decimation by 16, and 4 kHz bandwidth, the filter adds
about 3.10 bits.  An RTL-SDR therefore reaches about 11.10 effective audio bits and a
floor near 30 counts.  A 15-bit SDRplay result reaches the 16-bit container limit and a
floor of one count.  The exact RTL-SDR value follows the configured filter instead of
being copied from one measurement.

## Evidence and limits

A numerical check on 2026-09-19 drove one raw RTL-SDR least-significant bit through the
production converter.  The output occupied adjacent integer counts near zero, had a
standard deviation of 8.30 counts, and asked the scope for about 31.2 counts after its
percentile and headroom.  That agrees with the derived floor closely enough for a field
display and explains why the old fixed value of 32 looked reasonable on the RTL-SDR.

The calculation assumes that quantization error is uncorrelated across input samples.
Strong tones and deterministic converter artifacts can violate that assumption.  The
scope floor is a display safeguard rather than a measurement calibration, so deriving
it from the filter is preferable to pinning it to one receiver trace.  A live check must
still confirm that quiet input does not fill the display and that real signals lift the
trace off the floor.

## Live SDRplay check

On 2026-09-19, the RSP1B ran at 3.540 MHz with 14 dB gain, a 256 kHz IQ rate,
decimation by 16, and a 4 kHz audio bandwidth.  The scope settled at -72.4 dBFS on the
connected antenna, below the former -60.2 dBFS limit and above the new -90.3 dBFS
floor.  The input therefore selected the scale instead of either limit.

The second complete health interval reported no crossings past 100 ms of receiver
backlog, a worst backlog of 47.6 ms, and no clipping warning.  The first interval had
one startup crossing and a 216.8 ms maximum, which matches the documented startup
behavior rather than a sustained processing delay.

## Live RTL-SDR check

On 2026-09-19, the RTL-SDR ran at 3.530 MHz with 22.9 dB gain and the same 256 kHz IQ
rate, decimation by 16, and 4 kHz bandwidth.  The scope settled at -52.2 dBFS, above
its derived floor of about -60.7 dBFS.  Its trace looked the same height as the SDRplay
trace despite the different raw scale, which is the intended result.

The first health interval established a receiver clock baseline of -3 ms and produced
no clipping warning.  Both receiver checks therefore selected their scales from live
input rather than from the floor, exposed more SDRplay detail than the old limit, and
left the RTL-SDR display looking as it did before this change.
