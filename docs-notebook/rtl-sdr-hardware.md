# What an RTL-SDR Blog V4 actually does

Behavior found by testing rather than from a datasheet, on one unit, in September
2026.  Anything here that a second unit might disagree about says so.

## The transfer pool holds about a second

pyrtlsdr passes `DEFAULT_ASYNC_BUF_NUMBER = 0` straight to `rtlsdr_read_async`, and
librtlsdr substitutes 15 for it.  So the depth is exactly:

    pool = buf_num * block_samples / sample_rate

At 16384 samples and 256 kHz that is 960 ms, which matched a hand measurement to the
digit.  The agreement is what licenses computing it for other settings rather than
measuring again.

Two consequences:

**After changing gain, discard by block count rather than waiting a duration.**  Up to
`buf_num` buffers are filled or in flight at the moment of the change, plus one being
partially written, so discarding `buf_num + 1` callbacks makes every later block
provably post-change.  No dependence on timing, scheduler jitter, or the sample rate
being what was asked for.  Measuring immediately instead reads the previous step's
answer shifted by one, which looks like a plausible curve and is wrong.

**A sweep should open the device with a small block.**  The pipeline uses 16384
samples because it has a deadline; a sweep has none, and a smaller block makes the
pool shallow.  At 2048 samples the pool is 120 ms rather than 960, which turns 30
seconds of discarding across 29 gain steps into under 4.

Of the two factors only the block size is cleanly ours.  `buf_num` is a pyrtlsdr class
attribute rather than a parameter, so changing it means reaching into a library
constant.

## Changing gain while capture runs

Setting a gain is a pair of synchronous USB control transfers.  While capture runs the
capture thread is inside `rtlsdr_read_async`, driving libusb's event loop on the same
device, so a gain change from any other thread is two threads touching one device.

Twice in a few dozen sweeps that ended badly: a transfer never completed, and
`rtlsdr_close` waited on it and never returned.  Everything that closes a receiver
then hung with it, including the atexit path, and the next run met
LIBUSB_ERROR_ACCESS.  The monitor never shows this because it writes the tuner once
before capture starts; a sweep writes it 145 times during.

**Writing the gain from inside the callback does not work, and looks as though it
does.**  It seems like the obvious answer, since the callback runs on the capture
thread between transfer completions.  libusb's synchronous API completes a transfer by
pumping the event loop itself, so calling it from a callback that
`libusb_handle_events` is already running re-enters event handling, which libusb does
not allow.  On hardware the write simply failed, the gain never moved, and the sweep
measured a flat curve and reached no answer.

The trap is that it passed every test.  A stand-in device has no USB underneath it, so
the call succeeded there and the whole suite was green.  What now covers it is a sweep
driven through a real `RtlSdrSource` over a device whose level follows the gain, in
`TestASweepOverARealSource`: it asserts the measured curve actually rises, which a
gain that never moves cannot do.

What remains is the cross-thread write, with `close_device` bounding the hang rather
than preventing it.  The untried option is to cancel the async read around each
change and restart it, which removes the concurrency at the cost of a cancel and a
pool refill at every step, and `cancel_read_async` is itself implicated in the hang.

## Three artifacts that follow the tuner

All three sit at fixed offsets from wherever the device is tuned, rather than at fixed
radio frequencies, which is how they were identified: the same level appears at every
tuning tried.

    DC spike     about 37 dB over the floor   at the tuned frequency
    band edge    about 32 dB over the floor   at centre - 124 kHz
    pair         about 24 dB over the floor   at centre +- 10 kHz

The monitor tunes 50 kHz above the frequency of interest to move the DC spike out of
the measured band.  That offset also clears the other two, which sit 40 kHz and 74 kHz
from the 4 kHz measurement window.  **That was luck rather than design**, so recheck
before `tuning_offset_hz` moves.

The band-edge spur matters for anything that searches for the strongest bin.  Left in,
it wins every time, at every frequency, and looks convincingly like a signal.

## The gain labels

The getter is broken: measured on this unit, `rtlsdr_set_tuner_gain` works and the
level moves, while reading the gain back returns 0.0 at every setting.  So the only
figure anybody can know is the one they set, which is why the source snaps the request
to a supported step itself and records that rather than asking the device.

Whether the labels are true dB is **unresolved**.  One measurement put the full range
at 57.5 dB against a nominal 49.6, a factor of 1.16, and that figure is quoted in
`config.py` and `sdr.py`.  A later attempt to reproduce it could neither confirm nor
refute it: four proxies for the gain curve over the same span gave 0.99x, 1.03x, 1.11x
and 1.36x, each contaminated differently, and nothing on the band was strong enough to
settle the question.  See `sdr-gain-calibration.md`.

## The converter's own noise floor is flat with frequency

Measured at gain 0, the floor came to -91.2 dBFS at 3.530 MHz and -91.3 dBFS at
14.200 MHz.  A tenth of a decibel apart across two bands.

This is what makes an off-resonance run a fair proxy for the receiver's own floor, and
it also means the choice of control frequency barely matters.  The floor is not
entirely flat with *gain*, though: across the full gain range it rose 2.3 dB even on a
deaf antenna, presumably as librtlsdr redistributes gain among the tuner's stages.
Estimating the receiver floor from the flat region at low gain therefore runs a couple
of dB optimistic at high gain.

## HF coverage

The V4 upconverts for HF.  A V3 does not, and reaches HF only through direct sampling
with the tuner bypassed, which this program does not enable and which would break the
whole gain model if it did.  A V3 is refused rather than half-supported.

Development and every measurement here used one V4.  A V4-lite is probably fine.
Nothing else has been tested.
